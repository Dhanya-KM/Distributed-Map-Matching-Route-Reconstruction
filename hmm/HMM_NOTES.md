\# HMM and Viterbi Route Reconstruction



\## 1. Limitations of Classifier-Only Predictions



A classifier predicts a road segment for each GPS observation independently. It may select the correct road for individual points, but it does not always consider the connection between consecutive road segments.



For example, two consecutive GPS points may be assigned to roads that are not directly connected. This can produce an unrealistic route.



To overcome this limitation, a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) considers both GPS observations and road-network connectivity.



\## 2. Hidden-State Representation



In this project, GPS observations are treated as visible observations, while the actual road segments are treated as hidden states.



For each GPS point, the candidate road segments generated by the map-matching process are possible hidden states.



The HMM selects the most likely sequence of road segments corresponding to the GPS observations.



\## 3. Emission Probability



Emission probability represents how likely a GPS observation is to have come from a particular candidate road segment.



It can be estimated using the distance between the GPS point and the candidate road segment.



\- A GPS point close to a road segment generally receives a higher emission probability.

\- A GPS point far from a road segment generally receives a lower emission probability.



Therefore, emission probability helps identify which candidate road segment best matches an individual GPS observation.



\## 4. Transition Probability



Transition probability represents how likely it is to move from one candidate road segment to another between consecutive GPS observations.



It considers factors such as road-network connectivity and the distance or travel movement between candidate segments.



\- Connected and realistic consecutive road segments receive higher transition probabilities.

\- Disconnected or unrealistic transitions receive lower probabilities.



This helps the HMM avoid selecting road sequences that do not make sense on the road network.



\## 5. Viterbi Algorithm – Simple Example



The Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely sequence of hidden states by considering both emission and transition probabilities.



Assume there are three GPS observations: T1, T2 and T3. Each observation has two candidate road segments: A and B.



\### Initial probabilities



\- P(A) = 0.6

\- P(B) = 0.4



\### Emission probabilities



| Observation | Candidate A | Candidate B |

|---|---:|---:|

| T1 | 0.8 | 0.3 |

| T2 | 0.4 | 0.7 |

| T3 | 0.9 | 0.2 |



\### Transition probabilities



| Transition | Probability |

|---|---:|

| A → A | 0.7 |

| A → B | 0.3 |

| B → A | 0.4 |

| B → B | 0.6 |



\### Step 1: Initialization (T1)



\- A = 0.6 × 0.8 = 0.48

\- B = 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.12



\### Step 2: Calculate probabilities for T2



For candidate A:



\- From A: 0.48 × 0.7 = 0.336

\- From B: 0.12 × 0.4 = 0.048

\- Maximum: 0.336

\- T2(A) = 0.336 × 0.4 = 0.1344



For candidate B:



\- From A: 0.48 × 0.3 = 0.144

\- From B: 0.12 × 0.6 = 0.072

\- Maximum: 0.144

\- T2(B) = 0.144 × 0.7 = 0.1008



\### Step 3: Calculate probabilities for T3



For candidate A:



\- From A: 0.1344 × 0.7 = 0.09408

\- From B: 0.1008 × 0.4 = 0.04032

\- Maximum: 0.09408

\- T3(A) = 0.09408 × 0.9 = 0.084672



For candidate B:



\- From A: 0.1344 × 0.3 = 0.04032

\- From B: 0.1008 × 0.6 = 0.06048

\- Maximum: 0.06048

\- T3(B) = 0.06048 × 0.2 = 0.012096



\### Final result



The highest final probability is 0.084672 for candidate A at T3.



By tracing the selected previous states backwards, the most likely route is:



\*\*A → A → A\*\*



This example demonstrates how Viterbi considers both observation matching and transitions to find the most likely road sequence.
